**THE SEASON OF WAITING**

There’s an old story of a mother whose two young sons were a real handful. Like many young children, they were full of energy and insatiably curious. But somehow, these two always ended up in lots of trouble. Finally, in desperation, the mother decided to ask her priest for advice. After listening to her story, the priest recommended that she bring the boys to his office so that he could talk to them.

The day came, and she got them smartly dressed and took them off to the church. The brothers waited in fear until the priest finally called the older boy into his office. Once he was seated, the priest looked at the boy intently and asked, “Where is God?” The boy looked at him with big eyes, but he was unable to speak because of his fear. So the priest tried again, this time a little louder: “Where is God?” This was too much for the poor boy, so he shot off his chair and out of the office. Grabbing his little brother’s hand, he ran as fast and as far as he could. When they had managed to put some distance between themselves and the church, they stopped, panting and shaking as they caught their breath. Finally, the younger boy found the words to ask what had happened. Through his tears and trembling, the older brother blurted out, “They’ve lost God, and they’re trying to pin it on us!”

Sometimes I feel like Advent is a time when we try once again to find the God we have lost in the course of the year. Our approach to this season can feel like it is locked into ancient ideas and worldviews that are no longer helpful or meaningful to us. It’s like the God who is absent suddenly decides to “visit” the earth, “come down”, do some God-like things, and then return to heaven “above” until God is needed or decides to intervene again. Much of our default Christian language perpetuates this idea that God lives “up there“ in heaven and dips into our world from time to time to fix things, or to punish evil-doers with a plague or a natural disaster. We may speak about God being omnipresent, but it is common to hear believers speak of certain places as ”God-forsaken”, to say that God has “left” somewhere, or that the Spirit “isn’t here.” When we refer to God as Spirit, we often mean non-material, separate from the physical world, and disembodied. And all of this baggage turns Advent into a season of literally waiting for the Christmas celebration, when Christ comes to us again (as if he has been absent for the rest of the year).

But what if Advent is more than an annual ritualised experience of waiting for Jesus’ birth? What if this season is not just about the idea of God’s past and future appearances, but an invitation to experience the reality of God here and now, with us, among us, and even within us? What if what we’re waiting for isn’t to remember the incarnation of Christ two millennia ago, but rather for us to wake up to our own nature as incarnations of the divine? What if this season is trying to show us that we already have what we’re waiting for? What if, to paraphrase Rumi, “What we seek is seeking us”?

These questions bring us to the few short verses from Romans 13:11-14 which are included in the Lectionary for the First Sunday of Advent in Year A.

**WAKING UP**

After explaining how God liberated and reconciled the world to God’s Self in Christ, and created a new community to embody and proclaim God’s grace, Paul called the Roman Christians to offer themselves as living sacrifices (12:1-2), and to live in respectful, loving, and mutually beneficial relationships (12:3-21). He then explored how the believers should relate to earthly governments (13:1-7), and summed up the entire law, as Jesus did, in the command to love (13:8-10). He then added urgency to his instructions by saying, “You know what time it is. The hour has already come…”. While it is probable that Paul was expecting Christ’s return to happen soon,<sup><span>¹</span></sup> there is also a sense here that he is speaking of living in “the new age that has dawned in Christ”—the verb used here is the same one Jesus used to declare that God’s reign had “drawn near”.² So for Paul, the believers needed to wake up to the reality of God’s presence and God’s reign that were already with them and working in and through them.

The language in these short verses is baptismal.³ Paul reminded the Roman Christians of the new identity and way of being that they entered into at their baptism, and called them to embody their new selves in their lives and relationships. But what did it mean for them to wake up in this way? Essentially, it meant that Paul was not calling them to wait for Christ’s return, but to live as if it had already happened—to embody in their present lives, the future they were longing for. The ”new aeon” had already been inaugurated through Christ’s death and resurrection,⁴ and so there was no waiting to be saved or forgiven. The separation of heaven and earth, matter and spirit, God and humans had already been overcome. Waking up, for the believers, was realising the truth that was revealed in Christ—that all of creation was permeated with God’s presence and life, that everyone and everything is an incarnation of God.⁵ If there was any waiting to be done, it was the whole of creation that was waiting for “God’s children to be revealed” (8:19), for Christians to recognise the sacredness in all things and to embody the love, grace, justice, and healing of Christ.
