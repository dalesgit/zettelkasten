# The Appalling Campaign to Erase Jan. 6 Never Ends

Oct. 13, 2025, 5:03 a.m. ET

![An illustration depicting a calendar from which the 6th day is being removed by an orange hand.](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2025/10/13/opinion/13bruni-newsletter-image/13bruni-newsletter-image-articleLarge.jpg?quality=75&auto=webp&disable=upscale)

Credit...Ben Wiseman

[![Frank Bruni](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2018/04/03/opinion/frank-bruni/frank-bruni-thumbLarge.png "Frank Bruni")](https://www.nytimes.com/by/frank-bruni)

[Frank Bruni](https://www.nytimes.com/by/frank-bruni)

Mr. Bruni is a contributing Opinion writer who was on the staff of The Times for more than 25 years.

I guess Attorney General Pam Bondi felt that actually flipping Democratic senators the bird would be too much, so she let her sour expression and clipped expectorations do the equivalent when she testified before the Senate Judiciary Committee last week. What a repellent snit, staged primarily for President Trump, who relishes any surrogate who can sulk, scold and rage as contemptuously as he does. He doesn’t want good-will ambassadors. He wants ill-will amplifiers. Bondi got the memo and spread the bile.

But I found the behavior of Republican senators at the hearing even more disturbing, because several of them used their remarks to travel back to Jan. 6, 2021, and demand that Democrats — yes, Democrats — [answer for their conduct](https://www.politico.com/live-updates/2025/10/07/congress/josh-hawley-fbi-pam-bondi-testimony-00596258) in relation to it.

That, of course, was the day when Trump, insisting without grounds that Joe Biden had stolen the presidential election, pumped up a horde of hellions he’d summoned to Washington. The day when they then proceeded to storm the Capitol, assaulting law enforcement officers and hunting down members of Congress. “Insurrection,” “invasion” — call it what you will, it was terrifying and unconscionable either way. And in a sane democracy, it would have ended Trump’s political career as Republican lawmakers barred the doors forevermore.

But the lackeys at last week’s hearing continued to prop him up and to pretty him up, to pamper and to please him, by [misrepresenting federal investigators’ apparent examination](https://www.the-independent.com/news/world/americas/us-politics/fbi-republican-phones-arctic-frost-wiretap-b2841240.html) of Republican lawmakers’ phone records from the period around Jan. 6 as the real scandal. That morally perverse switcheroo was no accident and no one-off. It belongs to an audacious mission to turn Jan. 6 on its head — to erase the shame of it by interring the truth of it.

Why does Trump drone on and on about a rigged, stolen 2020 election in settings — for example, his recent speech to military leaders — where it has no logical place? Partly because he can’t quit his claims of victimhood, which have potent resonance in this age of grievance. Partly as a justification for his vendettas. But also because it allows him, his Republican abettors and the MAGA faithful to think and talk differently about Jan. 6. Sure, the rioters spun out of control, but can you blame them? Thieving Democrats and a cabal of their co-conspirators had usurped voters’ will.

I think that at some point, on some level, Trump understood that his complicity in the violence of Jan. 6 — his responsibility for it, according to the articles of his _second_ impeachment — was more damning than any of his other transgressions: an utterly inexcusable, definitively nullifying breach. I’m sure that some Republican members of Congress still, in the recesses of their dormant consciences, recognize it as such. That’s why they’ve questioned and recast Jan. 6 in so many ways, from so many angles, at such length. Trump and the Republicans who’ve hitched their political fates to him _must_ defuse it, confuse it, make it go away.

And to a shocking degree, they’ve succeeded. The outrageous actions by which Trump, at the end of his first term in the White House, sought to overturn the election results and cling to power are musty news, fuzzy memories, curiosities.

There’s an audio recording — his voice, his words, no quarrel over the authenticity of it, no mistaking its intent — of a Jan. 2, 2021, [phone call](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/08/14/us/elections/trump-georgia-election-audio-raffensperger.html) during which Trump orders Brad Raffensperger, the top election official in Georgia, to “find 11,780 votes” and thus convert Biden’s victory in the state to a Trump win. It became part of the Georgia prosecutor Fani Willis’s election interference and conspiracy case against Trump. But over time, that sinister conversation got less attention than questions about Willis’s professional conduct, about her personal life, about minutiae whose significance paled beside Trump’s intimidation tactics.

That was the handiwork of Trump and his minions. That was the plan: Use ancillary melodramas, blanket denials and sweeping counteraccusations to create a dense fog in which everything blurs together and nothing is clear. Trump impugned the integrity of Jack Smith, the special prosecutor whose investigation may be connected to the examination of Republican lawmakers’ phone records that came up last week. He went after judges. He combined all of that into a narrative of political persecution fashioned precisely for our tribal times, and he did that so relentlessly and so operatically — he was a bona fide martyr! — that it thickened the fog and no doubt left many Americans less and less sure about the actual facts, the real culprits. Would he be _this_ worked up about absolutely nothing?

Would he go so far as to [pardon or commute the sentences](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/01/20/us/politics/trump-pardons-jan-6.html) of more than 1,500 people charged with crimes in connection to the rioting on Jan. 6? To celebrate them as “patriots”? To [fire federal prosecutors](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/08/24/nyregion/january-6-capitol-riot-prosecutors.html) who pursued the cases against them? To [claim](https://www.politico.com/news/2025/09/27/trump-january-6-fbi-00583383) that exactly 274 F.B.I. agents in plain clothes were “secretly placed” among the rioters to egg them on? That last flourish was so preposterous that even one of the president’s most slobbering sycophants, Kash Patel, the current director of the F.B.I., [rebutted](https://www.cnn.com/2025/09/29/politics/kash-patel-fbi-january-6-analysis) it.

But Republicans generally never correct Trump. They coddle him. Exonerate him. Which is what the Republicans on the Senate Judiciary Committee did when Bondi dropped by. The tangent that they went on was no tangent at all. It was a purposeful, cynical distraction in the service of their irascible overlord and his biggest, most enduring lie.

## For the Love of Sentences

Image

Credit...Michael Falco for The New York Times

In The Wall Street Journal, Peggy Noonan [processed](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/the-embarrassing-pete-hegseth-e8e1e910?gaa_at=eafs&gaa_n=ASWzDAh0yjzOkXY3evncn20px2E-5u-0PMXFZ5DcxDXpualBuH-A_-b5mItVnqoso_A%3D&gaa_ts=68e3a9be&gaa_sig=txssuqi_VF1GA6boNhPqHJsD_KvmFgiPC-S78QOoWQnIODEisq_3TALY2Sil3FTGffblSsBPUuktAZ8xqFqtTQ%3D%3D) (and pilloried) Pete Hegseth: “When you are driven by a sense of urgency you must still try to act like a normal person — normal in your comportment, which means sober, judicious. Not like some pumped-up drama queen who makes everything more jarring and fevered, and who comes across as the living answer to the question, ‘What would it look like if Captain Queeg took Adderall?’” (Thanks to Linda Acheson Pool, Bonita Springs, Fla., and Marc Gershow, Manhattan, among others, for nominating this.)

In The New Yorker, Benjamin Wallace-Wells [reviewed](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2025/10/13/donald-trump-pete-hegseth-and-the-war-from-within) Trump’s speech to American military leaders shortly after Hegseth’s: “He proposed using American cities as ‘training grounds’ for the military, envisioning a ‘quick-reaction force’ that would be sent out at his discretion. ‘This is going to be a major part for some of the people in this room,’ Trump said, like a theater teacher trying to gin up interest in the spring musical.” (Jim Long, London)

In his newsletter, Michael Cohen [reacted](https://therealmichaelcohen.substack.com/p/heads-i-win-tails-i-also-win) to Trump’s announcement that a special $1 coin to mark our country’s 250th birthday would bear Trump’s visage on both the front and the back: “This isn’t just commemorative currency; it’s a daily intrusion. A two-sided idol stuffed into wallets, tossed in car cup holders, buried in couch cushions. Even Melania, who already has her fill, now gets the joy of bumping into him in spare change.” (Diane Buerkle, Hendersonville, N.C.)

In The Daily Beast, David Rothkopf [illuminated](https://www.thedailybeast.com/this-is-the-real-reason-trumps-a-disaster-merchant/) the president’s willed (and very dark) thinking when he looks at Portland, Ore.: “He sees World War III. Chicago, L.A., Memphis, New York, even D.C. — the same thing! He sees Democrats who frankly can barely organize well enough to produce a strongly worded bumper sticker and his response is, ‘It is an insurrection! I must invoke the Insurrection Act!’ (Which you’ll recall he did not do back in 2021 when we actually had an insurrection.)” (Lisa C. Stewart, Boise, Idaho)

At PennLive, John Baer [pondered](https://www.pennlive.com/politics/2025/10/all-rise-the-us-supreme-court-is-about-to-buff-up-trumps-golden-age-john-baer.html) the current Supreme Court’s relationship to Trump: “This is more than a friendship. More than a courtship. This is a marriage of like minds. I’d say circa 1950. And the court’s current docket looks like listings on a soda shop jukebox of all Trump’s favorite tunes. It’s a good bet the court’s response will be, ‘Hey, Big D., let’s dance.’” (Edwin Jackson, New Cumberland, Pa.)

In The Times, Natasha Sarin [warned](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/10/06/opinion/ai-growth-economy-jobs-tariffs.html?smid=url-share) that an A.I. boom was compensating for — and covering up — an otherwise weak economy under Trump: “The situation is worse than having all of your economic eggs in one basket. It’s closer to putting all of your eggs in one basket and stomping on all the other baskets.” (Lani Jacobson, Herndon, Va., and Dan Leemon, Atherton, Calif.)

Also in The Times, Parul Sehgal [charted](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/10/09/magazine/thomas-pynchon-shadow-ticket.html) the curiosities and confusions of “Shadow Ticket,” a new novel by Thomas Pynchon: “It’s true that Pynchon can construct a cathedral out of language, but he also seems to have no idea where the light switches are located.” (Rebecca Earl, Avon, Conn., and David R. Whitaker, Wilmington, N.C.) Parul’s take on Pynchon’s earlier work was more positive: “He unleashed a vision of America that now feels all too familiar — a world swathed in conspiracy and trawled by self-appointed sleuths parsing every passing signal and sign, their paths lit by the bright beam of their own righteousness.”

And Clyde Haberman [recalled](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/10/07/dining/saul-zabar-dead.html) the life of Saul Zabar, who initially intended for his work in the family’s food business, Zabar’s, to be temporary: “Instead, he became one of New York’s leading lox-smiths.” (Rae-Ann Fischer, Manhattan, and Alan Stamm, Birmingham, Mich., among others)

## What I’m Reading and Watching

Image

Rebecca Ferguson in “A House of Dynamite.” Credit...Eros Hoagland/Netflix, via Associated Press

-   Although I love literary fiction, I’m often too tired to read books whose full enjoyment demands keen discernment, but then I cringe at the shoddy writing in so many of the purported page turners that I pick up. I want plot and polish both, and Chris Pavone often delivers just that. “The Doorman,” which was published in May, focuses on one fateful day, one fancy apartment building on the Upper West Side of Manhattan, and the cast of anxious New Yorkers who live in or orbit it. There’s social unrest brewing and there’s violence in the offing — Pavone makes that forebodingly clear — but who will be caught up in it and how? That question drives the narrative and generates its suspense. While several of Pavone’s characters are overly tidy types — low-hanging fruit in the orchard of the rotten metropolis — and his social and political critiques can be blunt, he’s such a smooth, spirited and verbally dexterous storyteller that I zipped through “The Doorman” merrily from start to finish. Here’s my Times colleague Sarah Lyall’s [admiring review](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/05/21/books/review/the-doorman-chris-pavone.html) of it.
    
-   The director Kathryn Bigelow’s new movie, “A House of Dynamite,” which opened in American theaters on Friday and will be available on Netflix later this month, is about the 18 minutes during which what appears to be a nuclear missile is headed toward the United States. It’s not about any hostilities or intrigue preceding the launch. Not about the big bang. Not about the day after. Just the waiting, the sweating and the government officials who must figure out what — and what not — to do. That close-up, real-time, granular approach gives it a chilling feeling of authenticity. Oddly and frustratingly, the movie becomes less rather than more tense as it moves along, but it’s still a riveting ride all in all, a worthy heir to Bigelow’s previous military thrillers “The Hurt Locker” and “Zero Dark Thirty.” Better yet, “A House of Dynamite” distills the “insanity,” as one character calls it, of nations’ reciprocal ability to annihilate one another as the world’s amulet against annihilation.

Mary and I take turns. When we walk our dogs along our neighborhood’s woodland trails in the morning, I lead for a while, then she does, so that neither of us has to breach all the invisible spider webs, which, depending on their altitude, stick to our chests or shoulders or necks or even faces. If the web is high enough and I’m talking animatedly enough and I cross it midsentence, a strand sometimes slips between my lips. With a creepy-crawler in tow? I spit hard, I swallow harder, I pray. Thus far, I’ve felt nothing making a new home in my throat.

Evening walks mean different nemeses: mosquitoes. They’re everywhere. They’re insatiable. And what they can’t get enough of is me. I’d like to take that as a compliment: Lesser people are unappetizing to them while I’m an all-you-can-suck buffet. But that positive attitude eludes me when their itchy legacy keeps me from falling asleep and red bumps and blotches spread across my forearms and shins. Lepers have comelier skin.

I share the above because I’ve been dishonest. I’ve told a partial truth. I’ve written in this newsletter about the beauty of the forest, the singular peace I find there, its birdsong and its breezes — all of which is accurate. All of which I cherish. But I’ve edited out the winged predators riding on those breezes. The eight-legged saboteurs with their gossamer traps. We have copperheads, too, and twice one has slithered across the earth in front of me, no more than four feet away. I didn’t feel so very peaceful after that.

Nature, like so much else, is complicated. It beckons; it warns. It delights; it menaces. It calms; it inflames (at least dermatologically). Over there, a plush carpet of ferns. Over here, poison ivy. That doesn’t mean I’m going to stop turning to the woodland trails as a refuge, a reprieve. It just means I’ll keep, at home, a different kind of balm.
