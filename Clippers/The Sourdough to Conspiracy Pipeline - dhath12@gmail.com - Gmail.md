There is a joke among crunchy influencers: “I started baking sourdough, then I got chickens, then I bought land..and then I stopped trusting the government.”

I’ve watched closely over the last few years as this trajectory has gained momentum, wondering how breadmaking, something that for me has always been a source of community, connection, and a lesson in grace, has become an entry point for fear and anxiety.

For many of these content creators, the trajectory leads them beyond merely questioning our food system and into distrust of all institutions and commonly accepted truth. It’s not just grocery stores, vaccines, and public education they question, but germ theory, the moon landing, and even the fact that the earth is round.

I hope that you have not yet found yourself on this side of social media. But when you post often about both sourdough bread and your Christian faith, as I do, the algorithm assumes this is just the kind of content you desire. Its prevalence in my feed is overwhelming.

For many influencers, and the audience that follows them, the shift is subtle at first. Making homemade bread leads to curiosity about flour quality and the value of long fermentations, which sparks an interest in the ways highly processed grains have become the foundation of the American food system. Learning a craft that requires care and attention, and results in something delicious, encourages the baker to consider what additional skills s/he might adopt. These hobbies spark further questions about why such skills fell out of favor in American culture.

But our social media algorithms work in subtle and terrifying ways.

If the new bread maker is a woman of childbearing age, she’ll begin to receive content advertising cleaner prenatals and dietary tips for a healthier pregnancy, building on her skepticism of popular flour brands to foster skepticism of the vitamins and dietary recommendations of her doctors too. If she engages with these videos long enough, the algorithm will assume she’s interested in fertility content from other breadmaking women, so it will feed her even more—this time from women whose skepticism has kept them from receiving any prenatal medical care at all.

**“They lied to us about…” begin many videos. “What else have they been lying about?”** The “they” in question being the media, the medical community, or the American education system.

The average viewer might recognize the extreme nature of these hot takes and remain capable of making reasonable choices for herself and her family. But gross curiosity keeps her watching such videos, which encourages the algorithm to feed her more and also encourages the original creator to continue leaning deeper and deeper into the conspiracy rabbit hole.

The danger this trajectory poses to those who tend towards the extremes is obvious: _[The Guardian](https://substack.com/redirect/8e6639b5-6a1b-460a-8cd1-a2c00a4aeb00?j=eyJ1IjoiMW91ejZvIn0.xCUUX-G1mqOe_p0uPEybRD6sQoY3OtX97OlEZbEJxWg)_ [recently reported on the rising number of stillbirths](https://substack.com/redirect/8e6639b5-6a1b-460a-8cd1-a2c00a4aeb00?j=eyJ1IjoiMW91ejZvIn0.xCUUX-G1mqOe_p0uPEybRD6sQoY3OtX97OlEZbEJxWg) within the free birth movement promoted by influencers skeptical of all medical care, a [child in Arizona died of malnutrition](https://substack.com/redirect/61e1cabc-5ea5-4185-bde3-191819ccdea9?j=eyJ1IjoiMW91ejZvIn0.xCUUX-G1mqOe_p0uPEybRD6sQoY3OtX97OlEZbEJxWg) a few months ago after his parents followed a diet proposed by influencers that avoided all acidic foods, measles is on the rise thanks to the prevalence of vaccine skepticism.

But whether or not the majority of us are prone to this level of anti-institutionalism, the presence of this form of content in our feeds is damaging. The constant barrage of skepticism fed to us online degrades our ability to ask thoughtful questions and engage in reasonable critique. It primes us not only to question experts, but to assume malicious intent on the part of experts whose findings have later been found to be incorrect or incomplete.

The language we use to talk about our questions, concerns, and values matter.

**Our language can either foster in us and in others a sense of curiosity or of conspiracy.**

Curiosity, I believe, is a gift. It is deeply good for us to foster a sense of wonder about God’s creation and a desire to continue learning about the complexities and intricacies of our bodies, our food, and so much more.

In my teaching, I encourage those who get excited about baking bread and are curious why higher quality flour improves not just the flavor but the digestibility of their loaf. I eagerly share about the history of American white sandwich bread and the fear of contamination that led to its invention. I critique the ways early 20th century businessmen used cultural anxieties around immigration to market their bread as the safest, cleanest loaf because it had never been touched by a pair of human (read: immigrant) hands. And I share that we have learned in the century since that the wildness of yeast, introduced by human hands, and a long, slow fermentation are necessary to create a flavorful and digestible loaf.

As I teach, I engage in critique—but I gently correct those whose questions venture towards the realm of conspiracy. We were never _lied to_ about flour. We learned over time how our bodies respond to the technological developments in American growing, harvesting, and baking of wheat. As a result, some farmers, millers, and bakers today are adapting their techniques to address the negative impact these innovations brought along. What a gift, as it opens up to us new opportunities for accessible, flavorful, digestible loaves.

This week, the USDA and Department of Health and Human Services released [a new set of dietary guidelines](https://substack.com/redirect/e7f75e00-f181-4bab-b28b-bf2fd2c93e25?j=eyJ1IjoiMW91ejZvIn0.xCUUX-G1mqOe_p0uPEybRD6sQoY3OtX97OlEZbEJxWg). Since the 1990s, the dietary guidelines have been jointly published by these two departments and updated every five years. They serve as the foundation from which school lunches, military meals, and SNAP and WIC benefits are determined.

Because I am not a nutritionist, I will not provide a nutritional analysis of the updated guidelines. Many excellent and thoughtful nutritionists and dietitians are doing so already, while also addressing the barriers that prevent so many people from eating in this way—barriers that are only being exacerbated by the policies of the present administration.

Instead, I want us to take a look at the language used in the announcement. It is language designed to foster conspiracy over curiosity. The emphases below are added by me to highlight the purposeful use of language.

“We are _**restoring**_ common sense and scientific integrity,” the first page of the guidelines states.

“For decades _**we’ve been misled**_ by guidance that prioritized highly processed food,” says the newly launched [realfood.gov](https://substack.com/redirect/0d310932-e955-4801-91d1-4bd63d5e2b71?j=eyJ1IjoiMW91ejZvIn0.xCUUX-G1mqOe_p0uPEybRD6sQoY3OtX97OlEZbEJxWg) website, below an image of the 1992 pyramid that was discontinued more than 15 years ago.

“The government is _**now**_ going to tell Americans the truth.”

“We are _**ending the war**_ on protein”

“Our nation is _**finding its footing again**_…rebuilding a food culture rooted in health, science, transparency, and personal responsibility.”

The language of these new guidelines, and of the wellness influencers who have supported this administration, present all previous recommendations as anti-science, anti-common sense, and pro-processed foods. The language fails to acknowledge that the dietary recommendations have, for years now, emphasized whole, nutrient dense foods, fiber-rich fruits and vegetables, and a variety of both plant and animal based protein sources.

Additionally, the language is designed to foster criticism of previous research and recommendations (“They were lying to you!”) and to garner praise for the current administration—with the assumption that the general public does not know what the previous recommendations said. It assumes the average viewer swiping through the new pyramid on Instagram won’t recognize that many of the recommendations labeled as common sense were in previous iterations of guidelines as well.

Finally, it primes readers to write off commonly held nutritional advice as beholden to corporate interests, while overlooking the ways that this administration is completely beholden to corporate interests too. The truth is, all versions of the dietary guidelines have been shaped by the financial interests of the people who wrote them—and this new version is no exception.

The language embedded in these new guidelines is designed to bolster the sense of conspiracy that is already gaining momentum online. In doing so, it turns any critique of the substance of the advice into a partisan debate.

I want us to be able to talk well about food, health, technology, and our bodies. I want us to trust the experts in our midst. I want us to foster curiosity alongside humility, to assume good intent from those who have committed their lives to the study of health. This is the only way we can develop a relationship to food that is as joyful, restful, and liberating as I believe God wants it to be.

To do that, though, we must first recognize the insidious role of inflammatory language. It might generate engagement and mobilize a crowd, but the consequences are damaging not just to our souls—but to the social fabric as well.

### **Chai Golden Raisin Bread**

> ½ cup whole wheat flour  
> ¼ tsp yeast, instant or active dry  
> 1/2 cup room temperature water  
> 1 cup freshly brewed (hot!) chai tea  
> ½ cup golden raisins  
> 3 cups all purpose flour  
> 1 tsp salt

1.  In a large bowl, mix together the whole wheat flour, yeast, and ½ cup of water. Let sit for half an hour.
    
2.  While the wheat mixture sits, pour the hot tea over the golden raisins and let sit for twenty minutes. The tea should cool in this time.
    
3.  After the raisins and tea have sat for twenty minutes, strain the raisins from the tea—reserving both raisins and tea. Add the tea and flour to the wheat mixture. Mix until it comes together into a shaggy dough. Let sit for half an hour.
    
4.  After the dough has finished its second rest, sprinkle the salt over the dough and squeeze the dough until the salt is incorporated. Next, sprinkle the raisins over the dough. Stretch and fold the dough 12-16 times until the raisins are dispersed and the dough is smooth.
    
5.  Cover loosely with a damp tea towel or plastic and let rest at room temperature for 10-12 hours.
    
6.  Half an hour before you are ready to bake, shape the dough into a tight round then preheat the oven to 425°F. If you are baking in a Dutch oven, place it in the oven to preheat too.
    
7.  Once the oven is heated and the dough shows signs it is fully proofed, bake the loaf for 45 minutes. Let cool and enjoy!
