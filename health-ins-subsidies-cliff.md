# For Some Americans, the End of Obamacare Subsidies Means Falling Off a Financial Cliff

Earning just one dollar more could mean a $10,000 increase in insurance premiums.

The expiration of the extra subsidies was the issue at the [heart of the government shutdown](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/11/11/us/politics/trump-shutdown-health-care-costs-aca.html) this past fall, the subject of continuing [congressional wrangling](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/12/18/us/politics/health-care-gop.html) — and likely to be a [theme](https://www.kff.org/public-opinion/kff-health-tracking-poll-health-care-costs-expiring-aca-tax-credits-and-the-2026-midterms/) of the midterm elections.

New [data released Wednesday](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/marketplace-2026-open-enrollment-period-report-national-snapshot-2) shows that enrollment in the marketplaces established under the Affordable Care Act is down by at least 1.3 million people compared with last year, as nearly all those who buy their own insurance now face higher prices. People who earn more than the subsidy limit are a [small share](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/health-insurance-exchanges-2025-open-enrollment-report.pdf) of the market, but their huge price increases are having big impacts in their lives.

Why was the Affordable Care Act created with a cliff back in 2009? The policy achieved a few important goals for the legislators who wrote it. It limited the total cost of the legislation — an important constraint imposed by the Obama White House. And it insulated Democrats from attacks that they were handing out tax credits to wealthy Americans.

“It was what could be afforded as a matter of politics and policy,” said Rodney Whitlock, a vice president at McDermott+ Consulting. At the time, Mr. Whitlock was a top health policy staffer for Senator Chuck Grassley of Iowa, a Republican who was part of the early negotiations over the bill’s design and ended up voting no.

But the sharp cutoff meant that people close to the edge faced starkly different costs for health insurance. People who earned just under the limit were expected to pay only about 10 percent of their income for an average plan. People just over the cutoff had to pay whatever that plan cost.

In the early years of Obamacare, the cliff meant a cost increase of a few thousand dollars a year for a typical 60-year-old, though prices varied around the country. Younger Americans, whose insurance tends to be cheaper, often experienced even smaller differences. (The numbers in our charts above illustrate incomes and subsidies for a 60-year-old living in the contiguous United States.)

Dan Sacks, an associate professor of risk and insurance at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, described the difference at the time as more of a “kink” than a “cliff.”

“The expectation was that this would not be a hard cutoff for many people,” he said.

In the years since, as insurance costs have risen faster than incomes, that cliff has gotten taller.

Many of Obamacare’s original authors [came to regret](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/02/27/upshot/biden-health-plan-obamacare.html) the way they had limited subsidies. So when Democrats controlled the government during the Covid pandemic, they redesigned the system to smooth it out.

Temporary changes [passed in 2021](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/03/10/upshot/stimulus-obamacare-lower-costs.html) and renewed in 2022 increased the generosity of subsidies for low earners, making plans free for about half of people who use the market. But they also extended subsidies to people earning more than the cliff’s original threshold of four times the federal poverty level. Until this year, all buyers, no matter how much money they made, paid no more than 8.5 percent of their income for the typical Obamacare plan.

But Democrats [didn’t have the votes](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/10/02/podcasts/the-daily/government-shutdown-healthcare.html?showTranscript=1) in 2022 to make the extra funding permanent, and it expired at the end of last year. Insurers, facing rising health care costs and worries that healthier customers would drop coverage in the face of declining subsidies, also increased their prices by [more than 25 percent](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/10/29/health/obamacare-prices-health-insurance.html). Now, many Americans are experiencing extreme sticker shock.

Democrats in Congress tried unsuccessfully to get the subsidies extended, withholding their votes on government spending bills for several months last year, and forcing an extension [through the House](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/01/08/us/politics/house-bill-aca-subsidies.html). A few senators continue to discuss a possible bipartisan plan, but the details remain unclear, as do prospects for passage.

Jonathan Gruber, an economist at M.I.T. who helped the Obama administration estimate the costs of various policy options, said the effects of the cutoff have become more pronounced over time. It “was not a big deal at the time, but has become a big deal,” he said.

For very high earners, the impacts are still small. The typical cost of health insurance still represents a small fraction of a millionaire’s income. But people with incomes close to the cliff face substantially different circumstances. The chart below shows insurance costs as a percentage of income for different earners. In 2014, 60-year-olds close to the cliff faced prices at around 15 percent of their income for insurance. This year, it’s more than 25 percent.

Cynthia Cox, a senior vice president at KFF, a nonprofit research group that tracks the Affordable Care Act, described charts like this as a “shark fin.” And they are not unique to Obamacare. Many social welfare programs include income cutoffs, like those for disability benefits, housing assistance, subsidized child care and food assistance. Defenders say income caps are a way to ensure that scarce resources are devoted to populations that need assistance the most.

Because low-income Americans often use several such programs, the effects of these cliffs often compound, said Janet Currie, a professor of economics at Yale. “You can have people almost double their earnings and not be any further ahead,” she said, making “themselves ineligible for one program after another.”

Research shows that some people fall off these cliffs. Others endeavor to control their income to keep their benefits.

The threshold for Obamacare subsidies differs from many of these longstanding cliffs because it affects higher-income Americans, who may have less experience with such policies. Professor Currie said more people may be surprised by the change or unable to easily lower their earnings if they are close to the limit.

The loss of subsidies has already forced [tough choices](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/12/08/health/obamacare-deductibles-premiums-health-insurance.html). Some people have raided their savings or downgraded their coverage — or [dropped health insurance](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/12/18/podcasts/the-daily/health-care-subsidies-obamacare.html) entirely.

**About the data**

Our analysis is based on data shared by researchers at the health research group KFF, calculated using insurance premiums published on healthcare.gov and state insurance marketplaces for the 2026 plan year.

Premiums are calculated based on the price of the “benchmark” silver plan averaged across the entire nation for an individual 60-year-old. The national averages assume every state uses the most common 3:1 age rating calculation. Tax credits and thresholds are calculated using I.R.S. income thresholds for each year and the federal poverty level for the contiguous United States. Numbers for Alaska and Hawaii differ. Dollars are not inflation-adjusted.

Individual consumers may pay more or less depending on their location and the exact plan they choose. Calculations account only for federal tax credits and do not include additional state subsidies, available for some consumers in several states.

People earning less than the federal poverty level are not eligible for tax credits and are not represented in our charts. In most states, such people are eligible for health insurance through Medicaid. But in eight states, poor childless adults without disabilities do not qualify for either Medicaid or marketplace premiums. In Georgia, they can qualify for Medicaid if they satisfy a work requirement.
