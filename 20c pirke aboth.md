One of the reasons that Jesus words from the gospels can sound difficult to understand clearly is that they may have been intended precisely not to be clear. 

Among the earliest passages in the gospels, as we have inherited them, our gatherings of sayings. In the years around the time of Jesus, there were other such collections of sayings. An example is found in the text called the pirke aboth, The sayings of the fathers.

One of them reads like this:

> A well-known quote from Pirkei Avot, attributed to [Rabbi Hillel](https://www.google.com/search?sca_esv=5b71cfa89013eb9f&rlz=1CDGOYI_enUS806US806&hl=en-US&sxsrf=AE3TifORE43zMCWTf9W1y-LiN68l19xSHQ%3A1757972147639&q=Rabbi+Hillel&sa=X&ved=2ahUKEwiYjIrL3NuPAxUxmSYFHSzGHzQQxccNegQIJRAB&mstk=AUtExfAPu_ObvlRNlkupu7XJa-wEKkkBXikWiezC3LSwCU1dXFYj9gMC2AA6gm8hKugfaD7JWgSRxw-RXNfxHO4NTpPEJrwefCofCHLxdJdOiDsH1xAqR40jbdnr14A6qnlZ-W3O8LZLTx0_DEENoBlolHmGXiCmOD-ubHm0thyp3XlyzfE&csui=3), is "==If I am not for myself, who will be for me? And when I am for myself alone, what am I? And if not now, when?==" (Pirkei Avot 1:14). This powerful statement emphasizes the importance of self-care and personal responsibility, while also cautioning against narcissism and the need for communal engagement and timely action.

It’s not intended to be solved. It’s intended to be lived with. Chewed on. Wrestled with. And put into practice.
