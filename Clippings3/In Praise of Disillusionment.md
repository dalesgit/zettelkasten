### When illusions fail, we come closer to reality, where we are called to act with hope...

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Evf1!,w_424,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F84c50528-2ec4-4cc9-bfcc-4a83152cf796_1828x2366.png)

##### \[When I sit down to write a Substack post, I try to avoid rambling on about nine decades of life, as I’m sometimes wont to do. But occasionally, I find myself pondering a long, meandering stream of experience that took me somewhere that might be worth writing about…\]

**I. Tomorrow marks sixty-two years since the assassination of President John F. Kennedy.** Like most people of a certain age, I remember exactly where I was when the news broke: walking out of a used book store in Berkeley, California, with a text I needed for grad school. Within moments of the shooting, people up and down Telegraph Avenue were looking stricken and saying, “Have you heard?,” while others were saying, “No, no, it can’t be!” I was one of the latter, and I meant those words literally. In my mythical version of the U.S.A., this kind of thing did not happen.

> **I grew up in an affluent, all-white suburb of Chicago,** and came of age in the 1950s. America and its allies had won WWII, the economy was booming for white, middle-class families like mine, so it was easy for “my people” to harbor the illusion that all was well. People of color knew differently, of course, and so did some of my teachers. But very little of what I was taught made it through my bubble of privilege into personal awareness.

> **So at age 24, I lost my innocence—or rather, my ignorance—about America.** In the weeks and months following JFK’s assassination, I was one of many lost souls trying to get my bearings on a landscape turned hellish overnight. Horrors like those that happened in Dallas forced me to take an open-eyed look at my country, and realize how illusions had blinded me to the truth.

**II. I was, in a word,** ***disillusioned*** **about the U.S.—** and JFK’s assassination was just the beginning of that process.Over the next few years, I had to find my way through a decade featuring more political assassinations, caskets returning home from an unjust war in Vietnam, and American cities burning in protest against our longtime refusal to end racial injustice.

> **As the 1960s went on, it became clear** that only the most deluded would regard violence as alien to America. With my myth of America crumbling before my eyes, I stopped saying, “It can’t be,” and started saying (with Kurt Vonnegut), “And so it goes.” In a country where people like me used genocide and human enslavement to assure that we would survive and thrive, there’s no inhumanity that “cannot be.” If that sounds like rank cynicism, I understand—but cynicism is not where the 1960s took me.

> **Instead, I learned that disillusionment is key** to living a grounded life. To be “dis-illusioned” means you’ve lost an illusion—and that means you have a chance to live a little closer to reality. As hard as reality can be, it’s safer and saner to live there than in fantasy, and that’s where you’ll find the work that needs to be done.

**III. Disillusionment changed the course of my life,** and sixty-two years later, I know that the course change was right for me. By the time I finished my Ph.D., I understood that academic life was not my calling. As cities burned, I left Berkeley and went to Washington, D.C. to become a community organizer, pushing back on “white flight” and helping people embrace the creative potentials of diversity.

> **And thus began my second wave of disillusionment**, but this time it was not about the world: it was about me. JFK famously told Americans to “Ask not what your country can do for you—ask what you can do for your country,” and that’s exactly what I did. But I did so with as many illusions about my own capacities as I once had about my country’s virtues.

> **My work in D.C. was bruising, edifying, and well-worth doing,** exactly the mix I needed to learn lasting lessons about self and world. To this day, it’s clear that the work made a difference to the community. But after five years, I burned out—not because I was giving too much, but because I was trying to give gifts I didn’t possess. Driven by “oughts” that had little to do with my birthright gifts, I was trying to lead people toward a place I had never experienced at any depth, a place called community.

> **So I left D.C. and moved into a Quaker living-learning community called [Pendle Hill](https://pendlehill.org/)**, where I did a long apprenticeship in the realities of “life together,” the tools of nonviolent social change, and the inner journey required to keep me anchored in “true self.” Eleven years later, I moved on to start working as an independent writer, teacher and activist, the work I’ve done with a glad heart for the last forty years. And I owe it all to disillusionment…

**IV. Today we live in the shadow of a monstrous illusion machine called MAGA** that’s hell-bent on subduing us with an addled fantasy of American greatness. Against all evidence, we are asked to believe that there was a time when this country was perfect, and we need to get back to it.

> **What MAGA means, of course, is that there was a time** when white Americans—especially affluent straight white male Americans—were in control, and we need to get back to *that*. To up the odds of this happening, MAGA is stripping public records of all evidence that people other than their entitled few contributed to this country in any way. They’re also trying to make sure that school children will not learn anything that might disillusion them about the U.S., because young people who know the truth might turn into adults who agitate for social change.

> **Well, I have news for MAGA: the jig is up.** Your callous disregard of basic human needs and aspirations, your fear of diversity, your obsession with power and wealth, your endless lies, and your ugly Christo-fascism have many of us more disillusioned than ever—and thus more committed to reweaving the fabric of our civic life in support of a democracy that honors all its multicolored threads.

**V. I wrote the poem at the head of this essay** on the twenty-fifth anniversary of JFK’s assassination. I was almost 50, looking back on the time that had passed since that dark day, looking for a way to name what I had learned from losing my illusions and inching closer to the reality of self and world. As I wrote, it became clear that I had been called to join the millions who do the daily “weaving” of life on which so much depends. You, dear reader, are very likely one of them.

> **Seen through eyes unclouded by delusions of grandeur,** the world is at once a wonderful and wounded place, filled with tattered souls and communities in need of reweaving. Millions render that kind of service every day, without having a name for it. They do it instinctively as family members, friends, neighbors, volunteers, educators, healers of various sorts, citizens of a democracy. I’ve aspired to join them as a writer, teacher and founder of a nonprofit that gathers people in “circles of trust” whose intent is to reweave soul into the fabric of our common life.

> **“Weaving” is a good metaphor for the work of** caring for self and others. It’s slow, patient, persistent work that makes creative use of the tension we find on the loom called life, and it gives us chance after chance to create something of beauty from threads of many colors.

> That’s the kind of work required to restore our souls and reweave our democracy. Done with love day in and day out, it’s work that will make “both the weaver and the weaving true.”
