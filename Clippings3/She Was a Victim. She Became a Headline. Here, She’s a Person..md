**WITHOUT CONSENT:****A Landmark Trial and the Decades-Long Struggle to Make Spousal Rape a Crime,** by Sarah Weinman

---

Married women had rights to their own bank accounts and credit cards before they had rights to their own bodies. Oregon was the first U.S. state to pass a serious, lasting law against marital rape — in 1977 — but it would take 18 years before every state in the nation had such legislation.

It’s one of many jaw-dropping facts in Sarah Weinman’s arresting new book, “Without Consent,” which tells the story of the country’s first marital rape trial, and the long, arduous slog that it took to make spousal rape illegal — though even today, as Weinman points out, there are numerous exceptions and qualifications needed in many states to prove such assaults occurred. And we still don’t have a federal law against it.

## Without Consent

The book opens in 1978, in an Oregon courtroom where a young woman named Greta Rideout has accused her 21-year-old husband, John, of beating and raping her — though the judge instructs the jury to consider only the rape charge. The marital rape law, only a year old, is untested.

The trial became a global phenomenon, with reporters and onlookers spilling into the hallways. Charles Burt, John Rideout’s defense attorney, told the press, “A woman who’s still in a marriage is presumably consenting to sex,” adding, “Maybe this is the risk of being married, you know?”

He won an acquittal.

Despite the outcome, the Rideout trial marked the start of a national movement to recognize a married woman’s right to bodily autonomy. Weinman, who has written two other nonfiction books, edited four crime anthologies and writes a crime column for this newspaper, tracks down a host of second-wave feminists who have taken on the issue of spousal rape with relentless courage. (It’s particularly satisfying to see cameos by people in the dawn of their careers like Maxine Waters, who was a California Assembly member at the time, and the lawyer Gloria Allred.)

The high-profile trial also allowed a woman’s sexual history to be used as evidence against her in court, which one today still encounters in cases across the country. See, for example, Brenda Andrew, who was sentenced to death in Oklahoma for killing her estranged husband; the prosecutor dangled Andrew’s underwear before the jury, referred to her cleavage and called her a “hoochie.” Her case is currently under appeal.

Unlike his wife, so far as we know, John Rideout endured no sexual examination, not even when, decades later, he found himself in court — again on rape charges.

Throughout the book, we are told of lawmakers, thought leaders and cultural icons who object to the very idea that a wife can refuse sex when her husband demands it — making spousal rape, Weinman writes, “an unresolvable paradox.”

“If you can’t rape your wife, who can you rape?” asked State Senator Bob Wilson, on the eve of California’s passage of a law similar to Oregon’s in 1979. (Wilson insisted it was a joke.)

“Perhaps what goes wrong with some marriages is you can’t rape your wife,” quipped John Updike, years before the Rideout trial.

The problem is, it’s the kind of joke we’re still telling — like [the locker-room talk](https://www.nytimes.com/2016/10/11/opinion/donald-trump-locker-room-talk-and-sexual-assault.html) of contemporary presidential politics. And it’s just as stomach-churningly unfunny as we watch the overturning of Roe: Women’s bodily autonomy and equality are in grave jeopardy.

It’s all of a piece. As Weinman shows, the jokes persist — and so does the crime that is her subject, as well as the rare reporting of it, the difficulty of establishing evidence, the sexism of many courtrooms.

And in this regard, I found myself wanting more from the book. “Without Consent” gives us the history, the trials and news coverage, the activists and lawmakers, but we are in a moment in which women’s bodies are, once again — and still — up for grabs.

Weinman acknowledges the Dobbs decision, but offers little in the way of what it may mean, or what it may portend. By 1993, marital rape was technically a crime in all 50 states. Yet the lone lawmaker, Bill Dean, who voted against tightening Ohio’s loopholes, in 2023, was quoted as saying, “I personally don’t believe that a man, if he’s married and has physical relations with his wife, that can be considered rape.” What are we to make of this? What does it say about women’s fight for equality? I’d have loved more.

But Weinman is the rare true-crime writer who rises above the genre’s inherent voyeurism to interrogate our moral, cultural and legal failings. She focuses on the stories of the women behind key cases and crimes, allowing them the depth of hindsight, and the rich humanity that trials by their very nature flatten.

She also does it by having written a book that is years — decades — overdue.
